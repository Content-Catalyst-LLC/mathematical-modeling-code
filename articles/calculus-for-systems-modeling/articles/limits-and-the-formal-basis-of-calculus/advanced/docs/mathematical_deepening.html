Mathematical Deepening

This section strengthens the article for mathematically advanced readers by clarifying formal definitions, propositions, counterexamples, convergence modes, and numerical-analysis warnings.

Formal Definitions

Limit Point

A point \(a\) is a limit point of \(D\) if every neighborhood of \(a\) contains a point of \(D\setminus\{a\}\). A function limit at \(a\) is meaningful only through admissible points of the domain.

Metric-Space Limit

For metric spaces \((X,d_X)\) and \((Y,d_Y)\), the statement \(\lim_{x\to a}f(x)=L\) means output distance can be controlled by sufficiently small input distance.

Sequential Characterization

In metric spaces, \(f(x)\to L\) as \(x\to a\) if and only if every admissible sequence \(x_n\to a\), with \(x_n\neq a\), satisfies \(f(x_n)\to L\).

Uniform Convergence

Uniform convergence controls the supremum of errors across a domain. It is stronger than pointwise convergence and preserves more structure.

Propositions and Counterexamples

Proposition: Uniqueness

In a Hausdorff space, a limit is unique. Metric spaces satisfy this condition.

Proposition: Compactness

A continuous real-valued function on a compact domain attains its extrema. Optimization claims require the relevant compactness and continuity assumptions.

Counterexample: Pointwise Is Not Uniform

The functions \(x^n\) on \([0,1]\) converge pointwise to a discontinuous limit. Pointwise convergence alone does not preserve continuity.

Numerical Counterexample

In finite precision, shrinking \(h\) in a difference quotient eventually worsens error because roundoff and cancellation dominate truncation error.
